What is a Lewis structure?

91164 / LEWIS STRUCTURES / PART 01

Showing the electrons that matter

A Lewis structure is a diagram that shows how the valence electrons of atoms are arranged in a molecule.

It shows two things:

  • Bonds between atoms.
  • Lone pairs — valence electrons that are not involved in bonding.
KEY IDEA

Only the outer electrons matter

Lewis structures focus on valence electrons because these are the electrons involved in chemical bonding.

Valence electrons are those in the outermost shell of an atom.



The octet rule

Most atoms are most stable when they have 8 electrons around them. This is called the octet rule.

When drawing a Lewis structure, we therefore try to arrange the electrons so that the atoms have a full outer shell.

Like anything in chemistry, however, there are some exceptions:

  • Hydrogen is the most important - it only needs 2 electrons.
  • Berillium - does not need a full octet, is stable with 4 or 6 electrons.
FIG. 01

How to read a Lewis structure

The diagram shows three different things:

  • Atoms — the elements in the molecule.
  • Bonds — shared pairs of electrons.
  • Lone pairs — non-bonding valence electrons.

A single line represents one bonding pair: 2 electrons.

Valence electrons

91164 / LEWIS STRUCTURES / PART 02

Step 1: Count the electrons

Before drawing anything, we need to know how many valence electrons are available.

The periodic table tells us this from an element's group number.

FIG. 01

Finding valence electrons

The periodic table is organised into groups (columns) and periods (rows).

For the main-group elements, the group tells us how many valence electrons the atom has.

QUICK REFERENCE

Valence electrons

  • Group 1 → 1
  • Group 2 → 2
  • Group 13 → 3
  • Group 14 → 4
  • Group 15 → 5
  • Group 16 → 6
  • Group 17 → 7
  • Group 18 → 8

For Groups 13–18, subtract 10 from the group number.

Examples

  • Mg is in Group 2 → 2 valence electrons.
  • K is in Group 1 → 1 valence electron.
  • N is in Group 15 → 15 − 10 = 5 valence electrons.
  • Cl is in Group 17 → 17 − 10 = 7 valence electrons.
IMPORTANT

For a molecule, add them together

A Lewis structure uses the total number of valence electrons in the entire molecule.

For example, in H2O:

2 H atoms × 1 electron + 1 O atom × 6 electrons = 8 valence electrons.

This total is your electron budget. Do not lose track of it.

How to draw a Lewis structure

91164 / LEWIS STRUCTURES / PART 03

A reliable five-step method

Use the same process every time. The most important habit is to keep track of your total valence electrons.

STEP 01

Count the total valence electrons

Add the valence electrons from every atom in the molecule.

This gives you your total electron budget.

STEP 02

Choose the central atom

Put the other atoms around a central atom.

Hydrogen is never the central atom because it can only form one bond.

Carbon is commonly central when it is present. Otherwise, it will typically be the atom which is alone that is central.

STEP 03

Connect the atoms with single bonds

Draw one single bond between the central atom and each surrounding atom.

Each bond represents 2 electrons.

Subtract 2 electrons from your total for every bond you draw.

STEP 04

Complete the outer atoms first

Use the remaining electrons to give the surrounding atoms full outer shells.

Add lone pairs in groups of 2 electrons.

STEP 05

Check the central atom

If the central atom does not have a full octet and no electrons are available, convert lone pairs on surrounding atoms into additional bonds.

This creates double or triple bonds.

Continue until the electron count is correct and the atoms have appropriate outer shells.

FINAL CHECK

Does your structure pass all three checks?

  1. Have I used exactly the correct number of valence electrons?
  2. Does each atom have the appropriate number of electrons around it?
  3. Does hydrogen have only 2 electrons?

If yes, your Lewis structure is ready.

Follow the method from left to right. The diagram below shows how the electrons are accounted for at each stage.

CONTENTS

The basic idea

THE CORE IDEA

Molecules are three-dimensional.

The atoms in a molecule are not simply arranged on a flat page. They occupy positions in 3D space, and the arrangement affects the molecule's properties.

Two ideas describe this arrangement:

01

Bond angle

The angle between two bonds around a central atom.

02

Molecular shape

The overall arrangement of the bonded atoms around the central atom.

FIG. 01

Same shape, different angle

Two molecules can both have a bent molecular shape while having different bond angles.

So, naming the shape does not always tell us the exact angle. We need to understand what controls the angle.

FIG. 02

Different molecular shapes

Molecular shapes can also be visibly different. For example, a linear arrangement is different from a bent arrangement.

The important question is: what makes the atoms settle into these different arrangements?

THE BIG IDEA

Electron regions repel each other.

Through both the number of regions of electrons, and if these regions are bonding, will we be able to determine the bond angle and molecular shape. This is because these regions of electron density repel so as to have maximum seperation with minimum repulsion.

Electron regions → Repulsion → Maximum separation → Minimum repulsion

Electron geometry

STEP 01

Count the regions of electron density.

Before finding a shape or angle, count the regions of electron density around the central atom.

A region can be:

  • a bonding region between the central atom and another atom; or
  • a non-bonding region, also called a lone pair.
IMPORTANT

A single, double or triple bond each counts as one region of electron density.

FIG. 01

Counting the regions

In CH2O, the double bond counts as one region and each C–H bond counts as one region. That gives three regions.

In H2O, the oxygen has two bonding regions and two lone pairs. That gives four regions.

STEP 02

Use the number of regions to find the geometry.

The regions spread out to minimise repulsion. This produces predictable arrangements called electron geometries.

Regions Electron geometry Ideal angle
2 Linear 180°
3 Trigonal planar 120°
4 Tetrahedral 109.5°
WORKED EXAMPLE

NH3

Nitrogen has three N–H bonding regions and one lone pair. That gives four regions of electron density.

Four regions give a tetrahedral electron geometry with an ideal angle of 109.5°.

CHECK YOURSELF

What should you be able to do now?

  • Count bonding and non-bonding regions.
  • Ignore the difference between single, double and triple bonds when counting regions.
  • Match 2, 3 or 4 regions to the correct electron geometry.
  • State the corresponding ideal bond angle.

Finding the shape

STEP 03

Electron geometry is not always molecular shape.

Electron geometry describes the arrangement of all electron regions around the central atom.

Molecular shape describes the arrangement of the bonded atoms only. Lone pairs are not visible as atoms, but they still occupy space and repel the bonding regions.

A

All regions are bonding

The molecular shape matches the electron geometry.

B

Lone pairs are present

The electron geometry stays the same, but the observable molecular shape changes.

METHOD

Finding molecular shape from a Lewis structure

  1. Identify the central atom.
  2. Count all regions of electron density around that atom.
  3. Identify how many regions are bonding and how many are non-bonding.
  4. Use the total number of regions to find the electron geometry.
  5. Ignore the lone pairs when naming the molecular shape, but remember that their repulsion affects the arrangement.
Lewis structure → Count regions → Electron geometry → Bonding vs lone pairs → Molecular shape
Regions of ED Bonding Regions Molecular Shape
2 1 or 2 Linear
3 2 Bent
3 3 Trigonal Planar
4 2 Bent
4 3 Trigonal Pyramid
4 4 Tetrahedral
WHY THIS MATTERS

Trigonal planar vs trigonal pyramidal

Three bonding regions and no lone pairs give a trigonal planar molecular shape.

Three bonding regions and one lone pair give a trigonal pyramidal molecular shape.

This shows how even though the lone pair cannot be seen, it affects the resultant shape.

Electronegativity

THE CORE IDEA

Which atom pulls harder?

Electronegativity is the ability of an atom to attract the shared electrons in a covalent bond.

When two different atoms form a covalent bond, they do not necessarily attract the shared electrons equally. The atom with the greater electronegativity pulls the electrons closer to itself.

REMEMBER

Greater electronegativity → stronger attraction for electrons.

Why does electronegativity change?

Electronegativity depends mainly on how strongly the nucleus can attract the outer electrons.

01

Nuclear charge

A greater positive charge in the nucleus gives the nucleus a stronger attraction for electrons.

02

Atomic radius

If the outer electrons are further from the nucleus, the attraction between the nucleus and those electrons is weaker.

FIG. 01

Using the periodic table

We can use the position of elements in the periodic table to compare their relative electronegativities.

  • Across a period, electronegativity generally increases (the nuclear charge increases, increasing attraction to electrons).
  • Down a group, electronegativity generally decreases (more shells are added down a group, meaning outer electrons are further away, decreaisng attraction to the nucleus).

So, when comparing two elements, think about their positions on the periodic table before anything else.

METHOD

Finding the more electronegative atom

  1. Find both elements on the periodic table.
  2. Compare their positions.
  3. The element further up and to the right generally has the greater electronegativity.
PRACTICE

Polar Bonds

THE CORE IDEA

What happens when one atom pulls harder?

A covalent bond involves two atoms sharing electrons. If the atoms have different electronegativities, the electrons are shared unevenly.

The more electronegative atom attracts the shared electrons more strongly. This gives the bond an uneven distribution of electron density which makes the bond polar. A polar bond is also said to have a dipole (which is a charge separation as a reuslt of electrons being unevenly shared).

01 Different electronegativities
→
02 Uneven electron density
→
03 Polar Bond

Showing the Polarity

Because the electrons spend more time closer to the more electronegative atom, the two atoms develop partial charges.

δ+

Partial positive

The less electronegative atom has less electron density.

δ−

Partial negative

The more electronegative atom has greater electron density.

You may show a bond dipole using either δ+ / δ− symbols or a dipole arrow.

WORKED EXAMPLE

H–Cl

Chlorine is more electronegative than hydrogen, so chlorine attracts the shared electrons more strongly.

δ+ H ─────── Cl δ−

This is shown either with the partial charge symbols, or with the arrow drawn above with a plus at the end for the positive partial charge.

KEY RULE

Difference in electronegativity matters

The greater the difference in electronegativity between the two atoms, the greater the unevenness in electron sharing and the more polar the bond is.

Polar Molecules

THE CORE IDEA

A polar bond does not automatically mean a polar molecule.

We can now identify whether individual bonds are polar. The next question is whether the whole molecule is polar.

To answer this, we need to consider two things:

01

Are the bonds polar?

Different electronegativities make polar bonds.

02

Do the dipoles cancel?

This depends on the molecular shape and symmetry.

FIG. 01

Symmetry controls cancellation

In a symmetrical molecule, equal bond dipoles can point in opposite directions and cancel.

In an asymmetrical molecule, the dipoles do not cancel completely, leaving a net dipole, meaning the molecule is overall polar.

Symmetrical → dipoles can cancel

Asymmetrical → dipoles do not cancel

METHOD

Finding whether a molecule is polar

  1. Identify whether the bonds are polar.
  2. Find the molecular shape.
  3. Decide whether the shape is symmetrical or asymmetrical.
  4. Decide whether the bond dipoles cancel.
  5. If there is a net dipole, the molecule is polar. If the dipoles cancel, it is non-polar.
Electronegativity → Bond polarity → Shape → Symmetry → Molecular polarity

Exemplar answer

(i) PCl3 is polar. BCl3 is non-polar.

Both the P–Cl and B–Cl bonds are polar because there is a difference in electronegativity between the atoms. However, PCl3 has a trigonal pyramidal shape, which is asymmetrical. Therefore its bond dipoles do not cancel, leaving a net dipole, so the molecule is polar.

In contrast, BCl3 is trigonal planar and symmetrical. Its bond dipoles cancel out, so there is no net dipole and the molecule is non-polar.

Exothermic and Endothermic

THE CORE IDEA

Where does the energy go?

A reaction or process can either take in energy from its surroundings or release energy to its surroundings. This gives us two important categories:

Energy goes in

ENDOTHERMIC

Energy is taken in from the surroundings.

surroundings → reaction
Energy goes out

EXOTHERMIC

Energy is released to the surroundings.

reaction → surroundings
THREE WAYS TO IDENTIFY IT

Look for the evidence

You will commonly be asked a question in which you must identify if a reaction is exothermic, or endothermic. There are three observable characteristics that can justify your identification.

01

Temperature

If the temperature of the surroundings increases, energy has been released: the process is exothermic.

If the temperature decreases, energy has been taken in: the process is endothermic.

02

Enthalpy change

A positive ΔrH means energy is taken in, so the reaction is endothermic.

A negative ΔrH means energy is released, so the reaction is exothermic.

03

Bond energies

Breaking bonds requires energy. Forming bonds releases energy.

The overall energy change depends on which effect is greater.

The enthalpy change is a measure of heat energy relased per mole (moles will be discussed more in the 4th slide).

Energy Level Diagrams

THE CORE IDEA

Showing where the energy starts and ends

An energy level diagram shows the relative energy stored in the reactants and products.

The most important thing to remember is: the products tell you where the reaction ends, and the reactants tell you where it starts.

EXOTHERMIC Products lower than reactants Energy has been released. ΔrH is negative.
ENDOTHERMIC Products higher than reactants Energy has been absorbed. ΔrH is positive.
FIG. 01

Exothermic energy level diagram

An exothermic reaction releases heat energy to the surroundings, so the products have a lower energy level than the reactants.

  • Reactants are drawn at the higher energy.
  • Products are drawn at the lower energy.
  • ΔrH is negative.
  • A curved diagram may also show the activation energy.
  • The line or curve connecting reactions to products is a representation of the actual reaction.
FIG. 02

Endothermic energy level diagram

An endothermic reaction takes in heat energy from the surroundings, so the products have a higher energy level than the reactants.

  • Reactants are drawn at the lower energy.
  • Products are drawn at the higher energy.
  • ΔrH is positive.
  • A curved diagram may also show the activation energy.
  • Again, the line or curve connecting reactions to products is a representation of the actual reaction.

Moles and Concentration

THE CORE IDEA

Counting particles using moles

A mole is a measure of the amount of particles. It allows us to represent large numbers of particles with much smaller, easier to work with numbers. In Level 2 Chemistry, you need to be able to move fluently between moles, mass and molar mass. Molar mass is just the amount one mole of a particular atom or molecule weighs. It will be given in the question.

n = m M
REARRANGE WHEN NECESSARY n = m ÷ M m = n × M
PRACTICE 01

Carbon dioxide, CO2, is produced when fuel burns in an engine. A sample of exhaust gas contains 300 g of CO2.

Calculate the amount, in mol, of CO2 in the sample.

M(CO2) = 44.01 g mol−1. Give your answer to 3 significant figures.

n(CO2) = mol
Working
Worked answer

n = m ÷ M

n = 300 ÷ 44.01 = 6.81663… = 6.82 mol

Heat Calculations

THE KEY EQUATION

Connecting heat, moles and enthalpy

When you know both the number of moles reacting, as well as the heat release per mole reacting, it would make sense to just multiply these two values to get the total heat released (the below equation is NOT in your resource book).

q = n × ΔrH
q heat energy kJ
n moles of reaction mol
ΔrH enthalpy change kJ mol⁻¹
IMPORTANT

What does the “per mole” actually mean?

The r in ΔrH stands for reaction.

ΔrH describes the heat change for one mole of reaction. The balanced equation tells you what that one mole of reaction involves.

2H2(g) + O2(g) → 2H2O(l) ΔrH = −572 kJ mol−1

Therefore one mole of reaction means:

  • 2 mol H2 react with 1 mol O2.
  • 2 mol H2O are produced.
  • 572 kJ of energy is released.
Do not confuse “mol of reaction” with “mol of a substance”. The coefficients in the balanced equation matter.
REARRANGE

Choose the equation that matches the unknown

Find heat: q = n × ΔrH
Find moles: n = q ÷ ΔrH
Find enthalpy: ΔrH = q ÷ n

Keep track of the sign of ΔrH. A negative value represents an exothermic reaction; a positive value represents an endothermic reaction.

Bond Energy

THE CORE IDEA

Every reaction breaks bonds and makes bonds

Bond breaking requires energy, so it is endothermic.

Bond making releases energy, so it is exothermic.

However reactions will typically involve both bond breaking (of reactants) and bond forming (in products) hence, the overall enthalpy change depends on the strength and number of new bonds formed relative to the bonds broken. This can be expressed as an equation in terms of bond enthalpies.

ΔrH = Σ bond energies (bonds broken) − Σ bond energies (bonds made)

The bond energy is the energy required to break the bonds (hence we leave reactants as is, but have to subtract the product bonds since they are being formed rather than broken).

WORKED EXAMPLE

But-1-ene + hydrogen → butane

The first step is to count every bond that is broken and every bond that is made.

Solid 1: Molecular solids

STRUCTURE → FORCES

Discrete molecules held together by intermolecular forces.

Molecular solids contain separate molecules. The covalent bonds inside each molecule are strong, but the intermolecular forces of attraction attractions between molecules are weak.

Non-metal atoms → Covalent molecules → Intermolecular forces → Molecular solid
PARTICLES

The attribute that governs properties of molecular solids are the weak intermolecular forces. Explaining the properties below.

PROPERTIES
01

Low melting/boiling point

Melting and boiling require bonds to be broken so the particles can be separated. Since forces of attraction are weak, low amounts of heat energy are needed, so the melting and boiling points are low.

02

Poor conductivity

Conductivity depends on the presence of charged particles that can move. Since molecules are generally uncharged, there are no moving charged particles, and so these solids cannot conduct.

03

Brittle

Weak forces of attraction means low forces/pressures can overcome these forces and break apart the solid.

Solid 2: Ionic solids

STRUCTURE → FORCES

A lattice of oppositely charged ions.

Ionic solids contain alternating positive and negative ions arranged in a regular three-dimensional lattice.

The structure is held together by strong ionic bonds which are caused by the electrostatic attraction between opositely charged ions.

Cations + anions → 3D lattice → Strong electrostatic attraction → Ionic solid
STRUCTURE

The ions are locked into a lattice.

Positive and negative ions are arranged throughout the structure.

Each ion is attracted to ions of the opposite charge around it.

PROPERTIES
01

High melting point

Strong electrostatic attractions require substantial energy to overcome and separate particles.

02

Brittle

When a pressure or force is applied, it will cause layers of ions to shift, which can cause like charges to align. This creates a repulsive force (since like charges repel), which shatters the lattice.

03

Conducts when molten or dissolved

When solid, the ions are locked in a rigid lattice by ionic bonds. However, when dissolved, or when liquid, these ionic bonds have been overcome, which means that the ions can freely flow. Hence it can conduct since there are freely moving charged particles.

Solid 3: Metallic solids

STRUCTURE → FORCES

Positive metal ions surrounded by delocalised electrons.

Metallic solids are made up of a 3D regular arrangement of positive metal cations, surrounded by a 'sea' of delocalised electrons.

Metallic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between a these cations and the sea of electrons surrounding them.

Metal atoms → Positive ions + delocalised electrons → Non-directional attraction → Metallic solid
PARTICLES

The electrons are not tied to one atom.

The metal atoms form a lattice of positive ions. Their outer electrons become delocalised.

These electrons can move throughout the structure. And so the bonds between electrons and cations occur throughout the solid and are non-directional.

PROPERTIES
01

Conducts electricity

Delocalised electrons are free to move through the structure. Since conductivity depends on their being mobile chargers, metallic solids can conduct.

02

Malleable and ductile

This means they are capable of changing shape under pressure and/or a force. Layers of cations can slide over eachother without disruption to the metallic bonds throughout, allowing the structure to hold together.

03

Often high melting point

Strong metallic attractions require energy to disrupt. This higher energy requirement leads to high melting and boiling points.

Solid 4: Extended covalent networks

STRUCTURE → FORCES

One continuous network of strong covalent bonds.

An extended covalent network has atoms joined throughout the solid by covalent bonds.

Instead of separate molecules, the whole structure behaves like one enormous molecule, called a network.

Atoms → Continuous covalent network → Strong covalent bonds throughout

There are three extended covalent networks you can be tested on: diamond, graphite, and silicon dioxide.

EXAMPLE: CARBON

Same element. Different structure.

Diamond and graphite are extended covalent molecules made from carbon, but their structures differ.

Diamond forms a rigid 3D lattice where each carbon has a single covalent bond to four other carbons. This leads to each carbon having a tetrahedral shape. The strong covalent bodns make it a very hard material, and since all electrons are locked in covalent bonds, it cannot conduct.

Graphite is a 2D extended covalent network made up of hexagonal rings of carbon atoms. Each carbon is bonded to three other carbons, which leaves one electron which is delocalised for each carbon. These delocalised electrons allow for graphite to be a conductor. Between these 2D layers, weak intermolecular forces of attraction form, resulting in a layered solid with these layers easily able to slide.

SILICON DIOXIDE

Silicon dioxide also forms an extended covalent network.

Each silicon has covalent bonds to four oxygens, giving the silicon atoms tetrahedral shapes like carbon in diamond. Each oxygen has two single bonds to silicon atoms. SiO2 is mainly characterised by high melting points due to the strong covalent bonding throughout.

Solubility

THE CORE IDEA

A substance dissolves when the new attractions are strong enough.

A substance dissolving means that a solute (the solid) is mixing with a solvent (the liquid) to form a solution.

When a solute dissolves, attractions in the solute and solvent are disrupted and new solute–solvent attractions form.

Whether dissolving occurs depends on the relative strengths of these attractions.

Solute–solute attractions + Solvent–solvent attractions → Solute–solvent attractions

If the new solute-solvent bonds are stronger than the inital bonds, then the susbstance dissolves because the new attractions are greater. However, if the opposite is true, it won't dissolve.

APPLYING THE IDEA

Like dissolves like.

Polar substances dissolve in polar solvents, while non-polar substances dissolve in non-polar solvents, because the bonds will be relatively stronger between two polar substances, and two non-polar substances, than a mix between a polar and a non-polar substance.

For example: a common question is ionic solids dissolving in water. Ionic solids are obviously made up of charged ions. Water is polar: its oxygen end (δ−) attracts positive ions (cations), while its hydrogen ends (δ+) attract negative ions (anions). These attractions help pull ions from the ionic lattice and keep them dispersed in solution. Commonly you will be asked to draw this:

FIG. 01

Water attracting ions

Water molecules orient around ions: the oxygen end (δ−) faces positive ions, while the hydrogen ends (δ+) face negative ions.

Exemplar answer

The non-polar chlorine molecules are not able to attract the polar water molecules with sufficient strength to overcome the solute / solute and solute / solvent attractions and so chlorine is only slightly soluble in water.
